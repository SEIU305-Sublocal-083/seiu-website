01 What is happening, and why?

What is a strike?

A strike is workers stopping work together to press for better pay or working conditions. The aim is to move the employer toward an agreement. A picket line is where workers gather to make that action visible.

[9]

Learn why workers strike

Why are we preparing to strike now?

We have not reached an agreement. After Aug. 5–6 mediation, our team declared an impasse, meaning the disagreement remained unresolved. In results announced Sept. 17, 97% of members covered by the Higher Education contract who voted authorized a strike. That gave our union permission to call one. Local 083 delivered notice to OSU leadership Sept. 18.

The vote included members covered by that contract beyond OSU. Talks continue.

[1] [7] [8]

Read the notice and next steps

What do we want from a fair contract?

Wages that keep up with everyday costs, health coverage we can afford and protection for our jobs. When wages fall behind prices, our paychecks buy less. When health coverage costs more, less is left for rent, groceries and family needs.

Our notice identifies unresolved issues in pay, benefits, insurance, hiring outside contractors to do our work, layoffs and contract length. These terms affect whether we can afford to stay in the jobs that keep OSU running. Read the dated updates for the latest proposals.

[1]

Read what remains unresolved

Is the strike definitely happening? When would it start?

It could still be avoided through an agreement. Our notice identifies Monday, Sept. 28, 2026, at 6 a.m. Pacific as the intended start. Bargaining is scheduled for Sept. 24. Prepare for that start and follow written union updates for any change.

[1]

02 Understand what taking part means

Am I one of the workers being asked to strike?

Our notice covers SEIU-represented classified staff in the Higher Education contract who are legally permitted to strike. Other OSU workers may have different contracts and rules. If you are unsure whether your job is covered, send your job title and department to our stewards. A steward is a union representative who helps with workplace questions.

[1] [5]

How can I get ready without knowing every detail yet?

Save union contacts outside your work account. List essential bills and due dates, and discuss transportation, medications and care with your household. Separate money you have from support with unknown payment dates. Tell our union early about a barrier to taking part.

A household-first readiness checklist

Whatever questions you still have about the intended strike, you can take practical steps now to protect your household, records and access to information.

Before any strike

  • Save our union’s contact information outside your work account.
  • Download recent pay statements, leave balances and benefit documents you are allowed to keep.
  • List essential bills and due dates; ask creditors about hardship options early.
  • Review the unemployment insurance and Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) limits before building a budget around them.

Before the intended start

  • Confirm the date, time, covered bargaining unit and instructions through official updates from our union.
  • Use personal devices and accounts for strike activity, and protect confidential work information.
  • If you expect to claim unemployment, follow Oregon Employment Department instructions to apply or restart promptly and keep filing weekly claims. Waiting weeks still require eligibility. [14]
  • Keep copies of management messages and your own factual notes.

During a strike

  • Follow peaceful picket and safety instructions from our strike leaders.
  • Report hours and earnings accurately to benefit programs.
  • Talk with coworkers respectfully. Answer questions with facts, make space for concerns and never use threats or shame.
  • Send media or legal questions to the contacts named by our union.
Make your plan concrete

Five conversations to have before Sept. 28

  1. With your household: list rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, care costs and minimum debt payments. Separate money already available from benefits that require an application or have unconfirmed payment dates. Strike pay is taxable; the approved weekly maximum is not a take-home guarantee. [12] [13]
  2. With our organizer or a steward: confirm the covered bargaining unit, picket assignment, check-in process and accessible alternatives.   3. With OSU Benefits: ask for written coverage dates, eligibility requirements for your appointment, premium responsibilities and continuation options. Tell the benefits office about a time-sensitive coverage question; arrange a private way to share medical information. [15]
  4. With Payroll and a steward: get the timekeeping instructions for your employee group, the applicable pay period and how unpaid strike time will affect your pay. Keep your own record of time worked and absent. Do not assume everyone receives an unchanged September paycheck.

Oregon’s legal process

What makes a public employee strike lawful?

The Public Employee Collective Bargaining Act (PECBA) governs this process. For ordinary successor-contract bargaining, the Oregon Employment Relations Board describes these steps: [18]

  1. Direct bargaining. Ordinarily, 150 calendar days of good-faith bargaining precede a unilateral mediation request; both sides may agree to mediation sooner.
  2. Mediation. After at least 15 days, either side may declare impasse if there is no agreement.
  3. Final offers and cooling off. Final offers and cost summaries are due within seven days of impasse. Their publication starts the 30-day cooling-off period.
  4. Notice. A strike-permitted unit must give 10 days’ notice by certified mail to the employer and ERB, stating its intent and reasons. Notice may run during cooling off, but the strike cannot start before that period ends.

ORS 243.726 also requires an appropriate represented unit, good-faith compliance and a qualifying contract expiration or reopening. It excludes certain unconventional strike activity. [19]

Does every OSU worker have the same right to strike?

No. Our notice concerns strike-permitted classified workers in the SEIU Higher Education bargaining unit. It is not a strike call for every person at OSU. [16] Oregon law prohibits strikes in specified public safety and other units. Confirm your unit with a steward, especially if your job, appointment or employment status recently changed. [19]

Being represented by our contract, paying membership dues and qualifying for strike benefits are separate questions. Use our pay and benefits guide to identify what benefit administrators still need to confirm.

Protection and practical limits

If you are worried about retaliation

PECBA protects lawful union activity and bars employer interference, coercion and discrimination to encourage or discourage union membership. Report threats or adverse treatment tied to protected activity promptly so our stewards can assess the facts and response. [19]

Do not improvise an on-and-off strike. ORS 243.672(3) identifies sitdowns, slowdowns, rolling and intermittent strikes as prohibited unconventional activity. Follow our union’s instructions and ask before combining work and strike time. [19]

Keep picketing peaceful, respect access and safety instructions, and bring conflicts to a picket captain. A lawful strike does not turn threats, violence or property damage into approved strike activity. Questions about essential duties, probation, a second OSU job or return-to-work instructions need an individual answer from our organizer or legal team.

If management questions you or gives a direction

  1. Request representation when appropriate. Our posted agreement, Article 17, Section 5(A), provides representation on request during an investigatory interview you reasonably believe will result in discipline. Use the Weingarten rights guide and contact a steward. [20]
  2. Make a clear record. Note the date, exact words, witnesses and any direction. Save messages you are entitled to keep, and ask for directions in writing.
  3. Contact our stewards promptly. Do not wait for a situation to escalate. Ask about recording rules before recording a conversation and about any deadline that applies to your concern.

What was wrong about the tax answer at the meeting?

Strike pay is taxable income. IRS Publication 525 generally treats union strike and lockout benefits as income. Its narrow exception for a payment established as a gift does not make ordinary strike pay tax-free. Keep payment records and set aside money for taxes as appropriate for your household. [21]

The meeting’s suggestion that strike pay becomes taxable only after $2,000 should not be used. A payer’s obligation to issue a tax form is a different question from your obligation to report taxable income, including income for which no form arrives. Ask our union which documentation it will issue; ask a tax preparer how to report your payments. [22]

Hardship assistance needs a separate answer. We have not verified the tax treatment of each kind of hardship payment. Do not assume that a bill paid on your behalf is automatically tax-free; ask for written program guidance. The IRS generally counts income paid to someone else on your behalf as your income unless an exclusion applies. [22]
Oregon unemployment insurance

Apply when your hours fall, and keep filing weekly.

Prepare a Frances Online account now. When you become unemployed or your hours are reduced, apply or restart a claim and follow Oregon Employment Department (OED) instructions. File a weekly claim for each week you seek benefits, including while a decision is pending. Answer every question accurately. [24]

  1. Identify the strike correctly. OED directs you to indicate you are still working for the employer involved in the strike and select “strike or lockout.” [25]
  2. Meet weekly requirements. Be able and available to work; currently, striking claimants must complete and record at least one work-seeking activity weekly. Recheck OED guidance because this requirement may change. [25]
  3. Report hours and gross earnings for the week worked. Include other jobs and work before the strike began, even if the paycheck arrives later. Ask OED how to report strike pay or hardship assistance; do not assume those payments are ignored or automatically deducted. [26]

Health coverage, food and retirement

Confirm the details for your household.

Will my health coverage continue?

OSU’s published rule for benefits-eligible classified staff requires at least 80 hours of work or paid leave in a month to earn coverage for the following month. This is a monthly test, not a pay-period test. Ask how any exception or protected leave applies to your situation. [33]

The meeting discussed avoiding a gap in coverage. That is not an individual coverage determination. Before relying on a date, ask OSU Benefits to confirm your medical, dental and vision coverage, payroll deductions, paid-hour requirements and what happens if unpaid time continues into another month.

If a reduction in hours causes loss of coverage, PEBB’s COBRA notice describes continuation rights for qualified beneficiaries. Continuation requires an election and payment; ask for your applicable deadline and full premium. [28]

Ask for a written answer: “Through what date is my coverage active? What could change that date, what would continuation cost, and how will I receive notices if I cannot use my work email?”

Keep open enrollment on your calendar. PEBB says everyone must complete open enrollment Oct. 1–31, 2026, for 2027 benefits. Arrange access and ask for help before the deadline if work-system access is interrupted. [34]

Can I receive more SNAP because my paycheck stops?

A household with a striking member generally must have qualified for Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) benefits immediately before the strike and still qualify when applying. Reduced strike income alone does not produce a larger benefit. Federal rules include exceptions to who counts as a striker, so ask the Oregon Department of Human Services (ODHS) to assess your household rather than ruling yourself out. [29]

Use Oregon’s ONE system to apply for food, medical, cash and child-care programs, or call ODHS at 800-699-9075. Each program has its own rules. [30] For immediate help, use our food and housing guide.

What about PERS and my time sheet?

Unpaid time can affect more than the amount contributed to retirement. PERS says a reported leave without pay of 11 or more business days in a calendar month generally means no retirement service credit for that month. Ask PERS and payroll how the rule applies to your plan and work record. [31]

Keep your own record of work performed and strike time. Confirm the exact time code, deadline and submission method for your payroll system with payroll and our organizer. The meeting raised unresolved differences between Workday and EmpCenter; this guide does not assign a universal code or promise an unchanged end-of-month paycheck.

Questions to ask before bills come due

  1. Name the immediate gap. List food, rent, medications, transportation and care costs, with due dates. Separate money already available from payments you have applied for.
  2. Ask the provider what happens next. Find out whether there is a waitlist, an appointment or a document deadline. Write down the date, contact and next step.
  3. Plan for access. Ask about language support, disability access, transportation, delivery and whether someone can pick up food for your household.
  4. Tell our union what would help. Contact a steward or our executive team about a ride, an accessible participation option or a hardship question. Ask what is confirmed before counting on an arrangement.

How does this fit with strike pay?

Community services, union strike benefits and unemployment insurance are separate programs. Read our strike pay and benefits guide for the taxable-income correction, application requirements and timing. Do not assume an award from one program establishes eligibility for another.

Support and donation questions: Use the strike hub for current instructions, or email 083execteam@seiu503.org. Ask where supplies, volunteer offers or donations are needed before making arrangements. This guide does not confirm a specific fund, ride or child care service for your household.
05Why workers strikeHow stopping work can affect negotiations, the risks, and questions members should be able to ask.+

A strike is a coordinated decision to withhold labor. Workers may choose it when continued work under the offered terms is unacceptable and collective pressure could help secure a fair agreement. The decision deserves clear facts, honest discussion and practical preparation.

Concern about a strike is reasonable.

Lost pay, care responsibilities, student disruption and uncertainty are real. Our preparation should help members work through those concerns with facts, respect and concrete support.

The practical case

Why stopping work can change negotiations

OSU relies on classified workers to keep its operations running. Withholding that work can make an unresolved dispute more costly and visible, increasing pressure for an agreement. How much pressure an action creates depends on participation, the work affected, public support and management’s response.

This is an explanation of the strategy behind a strike, not a promise about its result. Our bargaining team still needs to negotiate the language, wages and conditions in a proposed agreement. A strong campaign also needs a way for members to understand and evaluate that agreement.

A strike has a legal process

Oregon’s public employee bargaining law sets conditions for lawful strikes, including completion of the required bargaining steps, an end to applicable contractual no-strike restrictions and required notice. Some public employee groups cannot strike. A general statement that workers have a right to strike is not permission to walk out at any time. [43]

Use our Oregon strike-rights guide for the process, and the strike hub for our current instructions. Ask a steward about your employment category, work location or individual concern before relying on a rule meant for someone else.

An informed decision

Questions our members should be able to answer

Bring these questions to a membership meeting, a steward or our bargaining team. A concern about rent, a proposal or a work assignment deserves a specific answer.

What are we trying to win?

Ask which issues remain unresolved, what each side proposes and what the differences mean for your pay and working conditions. Use the dated proposals and updates linked from our bargaining hub; a number recalled from a meeting may refer to an earlier offer.

What has happened in bargaining?

Ask what changed in the latest session, whether another session is scheduled and what would make an agreement acceptable. Read current updates alongside the legal process rather than assuming that one step guarantees a settlement.

What does my household need?

Review expected missed wages, bill dates, insurance questions and the timing of any support. Our pay and benefits guide separates strike benefits from unemployment insurance and explains taxes. Do not build a budget on a payment that has not been confirmed.

How can I participate?

Ask about your assigned location, check-in, accessibility, transportation and care obligations. Tell an organizer about a barrier early so our union can discuss options and confirm any applicable benefit requirements.

How will decisions reach members?

Know where to find official updates and how to ask a question. A pledge, an authorization vote, a strike notice and a tentative agreement are different events. Use the current announcement to understand what action members are being asked to take.

What happens when an agreement is reached?

Ask how members will receive the proposed terms, what voting process applies and who will communicate return-to-work instructions. Do not infer a return date from an informal report that negotiations are going well.

06What earlier strikes can teach usA U.S. and Oregon timeline, lessons from earlier campaigns and how to read strike statistics.+

Workers have used collective action to seek recognition, safer jobs, better pay and dignity at work. These examples show different outcomes and different legal settings. They help us ask better questions about our own campaign.

History informs our choices.

A past victory does not predict how long another strike will last or what it will win. Our current proposals, member decisions and Oregon public employee law determine the choices in front of us.

Selected timeline

Collective action and changing legal protections

  1. 1894: the Pullman strikePullman factory workers’ dispute spread through a railroad boycott of Pullman cars. Federal intervention and an injunction helped defeat the action. The National Park Service describes both the workers’ demands and the consequences of the conflict. This is a reminder that disruption alone does not guarantee a settlement workers want. [47]
  2. 1935: the National Labor Relations ActCongress established the federal framework protecting organizing and collective bargaining for many private sector workers. Government employees are excluded from that law’s coverage. For our public university bargaining, Oregon’s separate public employee framework matters. [48]
  3. 1936–1937: Flint sit-down strikeWorkers occupied General Motors facilities in Flint, Michigan, interrupting production during a campaign for recognition and improved conditions. The settlement brought recognition of the United Auto Workers. It became an influential example of coordinated action changing an employer’s response. [49]
  4. 1968: Memphis sanitation strikeBlack sanitation workers struck after two coworkers were killed, demanding recognition and better treatment. Their campaign connected labor rights with racial justice and drew support from Martin Luther King Jr. After King’s assassination, workers continued the campaign; the April 16 settlement provided for union recognition and a better wage. [50]
  5. 1973: Oregon’s Public Employee Collective Bargaining ActOregon enacted the core framework known as PECBA. Today it establishes public employee organizing and bargaining rights, dispute procedures and conditions on strikes. Some public employee groups are prohibited from striking and use a different process. The statute’s history notes identify the 1973 legislation. [51]
  6. 1995: changes to Oregon’s bargaining processThe Legislature replaced mandatory fact-finding with the impasse and final-offer process. Voluntary fact-finding remains available. The Employment Relations Board explains the steps, including cooling-off and notice requirements, that apply today. [52]
  7. 2025–2026: limited unemployment coverage for strikersSenate Bill 916 (2025) authorized limited Oregon unemployment insurance benefits for striking workers beginning Jan. 4, 2026. Eligibility still requires an agency determination; this is not automatic wage replacement. [53]
Historical tactics are not present-day legal guidance. Read our Oregon strike-rights guide and follow current instructions from our bargaining team about participation. An action used in a past campaign is not necessarily protected today.
Lessons for our organizing

Look at the preparation behind the outcome

These are organizing lessons we draw from the examples above, rather than a prediction about OSU’s bargaining outcome.

Shared demands

Members need to understand the issues and the proposed changes. A clear explanation of pay, conditions and rights helps coworkers and the public understand what is at stake.

Support over time

Food, income planning, transportation and regular communication matter because members have to sustain participation. Asking for help belongs in that preparation.

Members’ decisions

A campaign needs a clear way to evaluate offers, communicate changes and decide next steps. Stories of previous strikes cannot substitute for that information.

Be careful comparing strike counts

The Bureau of Labor Statistics’ current major work-stoppage series covers strikes and lockouts involving at least 1,000 workers and lasting at least a full shift or day. BLS began this series in 1982 and extended it back to 1947 using earlier data. Smaller actions are outside its scope; older publications use different coverage. A change in the major-stoppage count is not a count of every strike. [54]
